\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a language model answer from an
external document corpus. That corpus is ingested from untrusted sources with no
trust check at admission, so an attacker can poison the answers cheaply. When a
source is later found compromised, the operator faces an unsolved problem: how to
purge that source cleanly, without rescanning the corpus or deleting benign
content. \sysname{} addresses this by recording signed provenance for each chunk at
ingestion. Revocation then removes exactly the compromised source's chunks, at a
false-purge rate of \textbf{\PerChunkFP{}} against \textbf{\PerDocFP{}} for
document-level purging, and traceback becomes one content-hash lookup. On the
real PoisonedRAG attack over Natural Questions the lookup matches two forensic
baselines with no model calls. Because matching is exact, recall falls to
\textbf{\RTrecallDriftThree{}} under 30\% post-ingestion drift; \sysname{}
neither detects nor prevents poisoning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) answers a query in two steps. It first
retrieves relevant passages from an external document corpus, then conditions a
large language model (LLM) on them. RAG has become the dominant way organizations
deploy LLMs over their own data. Enterprise RAG adoption rose to
\textbf{51\%} in 2024 from 31\% a year
earlier,\footnote{\href{https://menlovc.com/2024-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/}{Menlo
Ventures, State of Generative AI in the Enterprise, 2024.}} and
around \textbf{70\%} of organizations using generative AI now ground their
models with retrieval systems and vector databases rather than off-the-shelf
models alone, a category that grew 377\% year over
year.\footnote{\href{https://www.databricks.com/blog/state-ai-enterprise-adoption-growth-trends}{Databricks,
State of AI: Enterprise Adoption, 2024.}} The RAG market,
estimated at \textbf{USD 1.2 billion} in 2024, is projected to grow at a
\textbf{49.1\%} compound annual rate through
2030.\footnote{\href{https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/retrieval-augmented-generation-rag-market-report}{Grand
View Research, RAG Market Size Report, 2030.}}
The pattern is consequential precisely because it carries high-stakes content:
government agencies adopt RAG to answer citizens from official documents while
keeping regulated data in place and citing the source of each
answer,\footnote{\href{https://fedtechmagazine.com/article/2025/05/how-rag-boosts-agency-accuracy-security-perfcon}{FedTech,
How RAG Boosts Agency Accuracy and Security, 2025.}}$^{,}$\footnote{\href{https://statetechmagazine.com/article/2025/02/what-is-rag-perfcon}{StateTech,
What Is RAG, and How Are Agencies Using It?, 2025.}} so the integrity and traceability of the corpus is a
first-class requirement.

That corpus is the attack surface. Because passages are ingested from the open
web and shared document stores with no trust boundary at admission, an adversary
who controls even a few sources can poison the answers. PoisonedRAG shows that
injecting only \PoisonTexts{} malicious passages per target question into a
knowledge base of millions of texts reaches about \PoisonASR\% attack success,
steering the model to an attacker-chosen answer~\cite{poisonedrag}. Carlini
et~al.\ show this is practical at web scale: an attacker could poison a fraction
of LAION-400M-class datasets for about sixty dollars, and can frontrun the
periodic snapshots of crowd-sourced sources such as Wikipedia that RAG corpora
are built from~\cite{carlini}. Standards now treat this as a supply-chain risk:
the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists \emph{Data and Model Poisoning}
(LLM04:2025) and mandates that deployments track data origins and
transformations, vet and sandbox unverified sources, and version their
data.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/OWASP/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/blob/main/2_0_vulns/LLM04_DataModelPoisoning.md}{OWASP,
LLM04:2025 Data and Model Poisoning, 2025.}}

This raises a sharp operational question that existing work answers only in
part: once a source is found compromised, how quickly and how precisely can an
operator attribute the poisoned chunks to their source and purge every chunk
from that source? Query-time filters such as GMTP detect poisoned documents at
retrieval. GMTP masks high-impact tokens and checks their probability under a
masked language model. It keeps no provenance and offers no
recovery~\cite{gmtp}. Two forensic tools, RAGForensics and RAGOrigin, instead
attribute poisoned texts after the fact. Both descend from poisoning traceback
in neural-network training, which traces an attack back to the responsible
training samples by clustering and unlearning~\cite{poison-forensics}. A bad
answer triggers them; they then re-retrieve the candidate passages and score
each one. RAGForensics prompts an LLM to judge each candidate poisoned or
benign~\cite{ragforensics}. RAGOrigin scores each candidate with a proxy LLM and
clusters the scores~\cite{ragorigin}. Both are accurate, but both are reactive
and spend many model calls per incident. None of these tools keeps the
provenance needed to attribute in constant time or to revoke a source in one
step. A 2026 survey confirms the gap: knowledge-base integrity and provenance
defenses remain sparse, and rollback is a thin, under-developed
area~\cite{ragsurvey}.

We close this gap with \sysname{}, a recovery layer over an ingestion gate. For
each chunk the gate writes one Ed25519-signed provenance record into the vector
store. The record holds the source URI, the principal, a SHA-256 content hash,
the detector verdicts, and a timestamp. Signing provenance per chunk is not new:
prior and concurrent work already signs RAG embeddings to their source
hash~\cite{VectorPin}, binds answers to signed evidence with portable
receipts~\cite{leray2025policy}, and commits chunk-level fingerprints in
issuer-signed Merkle trees~\cite{MerkleSpeech}. The contribution is the recovery
layer this record enables, which none of those systems provides. Two side
indices do the work. A principal index turns revoking a compromised source into
one command that purges exactly that source's chunks. On real
partially-poisoned documents this removes the poisoned chunks at a false-purge
rate of \textbf{\PerChunkFP{}}, against \textbf{\PerDocFP{}} for document-level
purging. A content-hash index turns attributing a suspect chunk into one
constant-time lookup, replacing iterative re-retrieval. On the real PoisonedRAG
attack over Natural Questions~\cite{naturalquestions}, the lookup attributes each
of the \NsuspectsE{} top-ranked suspects from the released \texttt{e5}
retriever~\cite{e5} with no model call, matching two forensic baselines on
identical inputs. \sysname{} is a recovery layer, not a detector: it relies on
an external detector or analyst to flag the compromised source, and it neither
detects nor prevents poisoning.

This paper makes three contributions.
\textbf{(C1)} Surgical one-command source revocation: a principal-indexed
batch purge that removes exactly a compromised principal's chunks, evaluated
against document-level purging for false-purge rate and for mean-time-to-remediation
at corpus scale (E1, E3; Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\textbf{(C2)} Traceback reduced to one constant-time content-hash lookup over
the signed records, matched against two forensic baselines on identical
third-party inputs and stress-tested under post-ingestion drift (E2,
Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\textbf{(C3)} A reproducible open artifact (Section~\ref{sec:limits}) with a
digest-pinned real corpus, third-party attack and baseline code, and an
end-to-end harness.

\section{Related Work}

We position \sysname{} against five lines of work, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:related}. \textbf{Detection and query-time filtering.} GMTP
filters poisoned documents by masking high-impact tokens and checking their
probability under a masked language model, filtering over 90\% of poisoned
content at retrieval~\cite{gmtp}; RevPRAG detects poisoned responses at inference
time from LLM activations~\cite{revprag}; RAGPart and RAGMask are retrieval-stage
defenses that partition or flag retrieved content~\cite{ragpartmask}. These
mitigate the \emph{effect} of an attack but keep no provenance, perform no
attribution, and offer no recovery; they are a complementary query-time layer,
and on detector accuracy a filter like GMTP is the stronger tool. \sysname{}
targets the operational axis those tools do not address and claims no novelty in
detection. RevPRAG and GMTP act at retrieval/inference time, not at forensic
time over a fixed suspect list, so they are not run as attributors here.

\textbf{Forensic attribution.} Poison Forensics established pipeline-level
traceback. It traces a poisoning attack back to the responsible training samples
by clustering and unlearning-based pruning~\cite{poison-forensics}. The
RAG-specific attributors build on this lineage. RAGForensics introduced traceback
for RAG: it re-retrieves candidate texts and prompts an LLM to judge which are
poisoned~\cite{ragforensics}. RAGOrigin extends this to black-box responsibility
attribution with adaptive scope and rank, relevance, and influence
scoring~\cite{ragorigin}. All three report strong accuracy. All three are also
reactive, triggered per bad response, and their per-candidate scoring is costly
at forensic time. None attests provenance at ingestion, and none revokes a
source. \sysname{} is complementary. It pays a small one-time signing cost at
ingestion, so attribution later becomes a constant-time lookup, and it adds the
missing revocation step. We run both RAG attributors as baselines on identical
suspects in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\textbf{Ingestion-time attestation.} Generic supply-chain signing analogues sign
at file granularity, carry no detector verdicts, and are not instantiated for the
fragments that RAG retrieves: Sigstore/Cosign signs whole
artifacts,\footnote{\href{https://edu.chainguard.dev/open-source/sigstore/cosign/how-to-sign-an-sbom-with-cosign/}{Chainguard,
Sign an SBOM with Cosign, 2024.}} as does the CycloneDX
ML-BOM. D-RAG admits content
with provenance and human expert verification on a blockchain, but at document
or source granularity, with heavy machinery and no one-command
purge.\footnote{\href{https://aircconline.com/csit/papers/vol15/csit150417.pdf}{D-RAG:
Decentralized RAG using Blockchain, CSIT, 2025.}} \sysname{} is orthogonal: per-chunk granularity, a real public-key
signature, and the recovery layer of traceback plus revocation that the analogy
implies, as the rightmost columns of Table~\ref{tab:related} make explicit.

\textbf{Cryptographic provenance and receipts.} Several systems sign provenance
into the RAG pipeline. This is why per-chunk signed provenance is not where our
novelty lies. VectorPin, concurrent with our work, signs each RAG embedding to
its source-content hash with an Ed25519 signature. This gives tamper-evidence
against embedding-store exfiltration, but stops at per-embedding integrity with
no traceback or revocation~\cite{VectorPin}. Policy-governed RAG binds each
answer to anchored source evidence and emits portable compliance
receipts~\cite{leray2025policy}. ZKPROV uses zero-knowledge proofs to verify that
a response came from a certified dataset, at dataset
granularity~\cite{ZKPROV}. In the speech domain, MerkleSpeech commits chunk-level
fingerprints in an issuer-signed Merkle tree~\cite{MerkleSpeech}. Each
establishes signed provenance at some granularity. None pairs it with
constant-time content-hash traceback and one-command source revocation over a
text RAG corpus. \sysname{} uses per-chunk signed provenance as the means and
contributes that recovery layer.

\textbf{Provenance defense-in-depth.} The closest prior work, RAGShield, also
treats corpus poisoning as a supply-chain problem. It attests documents at
ingestion, weights retrieval by trust tier, and flags cross-source
contradictions, reporting 0.0\% attack success on a five-tier poisoning
benchmark~\cite{ragshield}. The same evaluation reports a blind spot: an in-place
replacement attack still reaches 17.5\% attack success. RAGShield signs at
document granularity, not per chunk, and provides neither signature-keyed
traceback nor source revocation, the gap Table~\ref{tab:related} makes explicit.
We compare capabilities rather than run it head to head, because it releases no
artifact.

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

\sysname{} is an ingestion gate (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). For each chunk it
computes a SHA-256 content hash, runs a best-effort detector suite, assembles a
canonical byte string over the provenance tuple (chunk id, source URI,
principal, content hash, detector verdicts, timestamp, signer identity,
granularity), signs it with Ed25519, and writes the signed record into the
datastore. The datastore maintains two side indices. A content-hash index maps a
chunk hash to its signed record. An exact content-hash match then resolves a
suspect chunk to its recorded principal in constant time. This exact-hash match
is the traceback operation. A principal index maps a principal to its set of
chunk ids, so revoking a source enumerates exactly its chunks without scanning
the corpus. The signed message binds both the verdicts and the content hash. A
record therefore cannot be replayed for different content or verdicts without
invalidating the signature, and any holder of the public key can verify it.
The prototype is implemented in Python using the \texttt{cryptography} library
for Ed25519 and SHA-256, and is organized as four components: an ingestion gate,
a signer, a content-hash index for traceback, and a principal index for
revocation.

\textbf{Threat model and scope.} The attacker controls or compromises a web
source ingested by the corpus and injects poisoned texts, including the
split-view/frontrunning case where a source mutates after
collection~\cite{poisonedrag,carlini}. The protected asset is the integrity and
\emph{traceability} of the indexed corpus; the secondary asset is
mean-time-to-remediation after a source is found compromised. The operator is
trusted and holds the private key; ingestion is where provenance is sealed.
Detection at ingestion is best-effort and may have false negatives, so
\sysname{}'s value is forensic and operational; query-time
defenses~\cite{gmtp,ragpartmask} are a complementary layer.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

We run four experiments on the datasets of Table~\ref{tab:data}, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:exps}. \textbf{E1} validates the instrument on a labelled corpus,
including exact-principal purging with no collateral. \textbf{E3} measures the
surgical revocation against document-level purging for false-purge rate and
mean-time-to-remediation, with per-chunk ingestion overhead at corpus scale (the
lead result, delivering C1). \textbf{E2} compares forensic-time attribution on
identical real suspects against two baselines (delivering C2), and measures
recall under post-ingestion drift. \textbf{E4} confirms the suspects are
genuinely dangerous via attack success. The attack, retrieval, ground-truth
labels, and baselines are all third-party artifacts that \sysname{} did not
author, so the suspect set used for attribution is independent of the mechanism
under test.

\textbf{E1: Instrument validation.} E1 checks that the gate, signature
verification, and the revocation index behave as specified before any real-data
comparison. The corpus is a seeded, labelled synthetic set of \EZeroChunks{}
chunks across five principals, one designated the attacker; ten percent of the
chunks (\EZeroPurged{}) carry adversarial text and are attributed only to that
attacker principal, so the principal's expected purge set is known by
construction. Each chunk is ingested through the gate and signed. We then run
three checks. First, we verify every stored record against the public key, and
verify that the recorded signature rejects a substituted message, which tests
tamper detection. Second, we revoke the attacker principal and compare the
purged chunk-id set against the known set of that principal's chunks; collateral
removal is any purged chunk outside that set. On this corpus all records verify,
the tampered message is rejected, and revocation purges exactly the targeted
principal's \EZeroPurged{} chunks with zero collateral. These are deterministic
correctness properties of the design, not detection rates.

\textbf{E2: Forensic-time attribution on the real attack.} The clean substrate is
the BEIR~\cite{beir} Natural Questions corpus~\cite{naturalquestions}. The attack
is PoisonedRAG's released adversarial passages~\cite{poisonedrag}. The suspects
and labels come from the released RAGOrigin attack-feedback file: for each of
\Nquestions{} target questions, the contexts the \texttt{e5} dense
retriever~\cite{e5} surfaced, each labelled poisoned or clean by the third party.
For each question we ingest its retrieved contexts through the \sysname{} gate,
signing each chunk with real Ed25519. The suspects are then the top-\TopK{}
retrieved contexts per question (\NsuspectsE{} suspects: \NpoisonE{} poisoned,
\NcleanE{} clean), exactly what an attacked pipeline feeds the LLM. We run three
attributors on the \emph{identical} \NsuspectsE{} suspects, all here. The first
is \sysname{}'s constant-time content-hash lookup. The second is the published
RAGForensics LLM-judge loop over a local \BLjudge{} model, one model call per
context. The third is the published RAGOrigin responsibility scoring over a local
\ROproxy{} proxy, which combines answer-loss, question-loss, and retrieval score
with z-normalization and K-means thresholding, two proxy calls per context.

precision does not apply to it; what does matter is how the lookup degrades once
the stored bytes no longer match, which we measure next.

\textbf{E2-drift: recall under post-ingestion mutation.} \sysname{} resolves a
suspect only if its bytes were admitted through the gate, so a poisoned chunk
whose retrieved bytes drift after ingestion (a paraphrase, the split-view
case~\cite{carlini}) will not hash-match. Paraphrasing a fraction $p$ of the
poisoned suspects after ingestion drops recall from \RTrecall{} at $p{=}0$ to
\textbf{\RTrecallDriftThree{}} (95\%~CI
[\RTrecallDriftThreeLo{},\,\RTrecallDriftThreeHi{}]) at $p{=}0.3$ and
\textbf{\RTrecallDriftFive{}} (95\%~CI
[\RTrecallDriftFiveLo{},\,\RTrecallDriftFiveHi{}]) at $p{=}0.5$
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}a). Precision stays at \RTprecision{} because a hash match
is exact; the missed chunks are false negatives. This is exactly the regime a
competent, content-mutating adversary operates in~\cite{carlini}: recovering
drifted variants requires near-duplicate or semantic matching, for instance
MinHash~\cite{broder1997minhash}, SimHash~\cite{charikar2002simhash}, or
locality-sensitive hashing~\cite{indyk1998lsh}, and is future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{E3: Surgical revocation, then cost at scale.} The lead result is that
per-chunk revocation purges a compromised source without collateral. On
\EThreeDocs{} real partially-poisoned documents (real NQ passages with real
PoisonedRAG passages injected under one principal), purging at document
granularity removes whole documents and so over-purges their benign fragments at
a false-purge rate of \textbf{\PerDocFP{}} (95\%~CI
[\PerDocFPLo{},\,\PerDocFPHi{}], \PerDocFalse{} of \PerDocPurged{} removed chunks
were clean), whereas \sysname{}'s per-chunk principal index localises and removes
exactly the poisoned chunks at a false-purge rate of \textbf{\PerChunkFP{}}. This
clean excision, not the speed, is what the recovery layer buys.

The recovery operation is also cheap. We treat one PoisonedRAG attacker principal
as compromised and remove it two ways on the real corpus. The fair comparison is
keeping a provenance index versus not keeping one. With the index, revocation is
an $O(1)$ lookup of the principal's \RevChunks{} chunk ids and their deletion,
\FullRevMttr~$\mu$s on the full \FullChunks{}-chunk corpus. Without it, a scan
over every chunk takes \FullManMttr~ms. The \FullMttrRatio$\times$ gap is just
$O(1)$ versus $O(\text{corpus})$ work, so it grows with corpus size: from
\SmallMttrRatio$\times$ at \SmallPassages{} passages to \FullMttrRatio$\times$ at
the full corpus (Figure~\ref{fig:results}b). The surgical purge stays flat while
the manual scan tracks the corpus, so the advantage widens as the corpus grows.
The index is paid for once, at ingestion. Per-chunk Ed25519 signing takes about
\EdLatency~$\mu$s ($\sim$\EdThroughput{} chunks/s, single-threaded) and produces
a fixed \EdSig{}-byte signature in an \EdRecord{}-byte record. That is about
\EdOverHmac$\times$ the cost of a symmetric HMAC stand-in (\HmacLatency~$\mu$s,
\HmacSig{}-byte tag), in exchange for non-repudiable provenance that anyone can
verify without the secret key.

\textbf{E4: Attack-success context.} To confirm the suspects are genuinely
dangerous, we feed the top-\AsrTopK{} retrieved contexts to a local \AsrGen{}
generation model and check the answer: the attack steers it to the attacker's
target answer in \textbf{\AsrPoint\%} of the \AsrN{} questions (95\%~CI
[\AsrLo{},\,\AsrHi{}]). This confirms that the chunks \sysname{} attributes do
mislead the pipeline it protects.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Scope: what \sysname{} is and is not.} \sysname{} is a recovery layer.
Given a source that has been flagged compromised, it attributes that source's
chunks and removes them cleanly. It does not detect or prevent poisoning. It
relies on an external detector or a human analyst to flag the source in the first
place. The ingestion detectors are best-effort and out of scope, and query-time
defenses~\cite{gmtp,ragpartmask} remain a complementary layer. Attribution rests
on an exact content-hash match, so it does not hold against a content-mutating
adversary. Any post-ingestion paraphrase or split-view
mutation~\cite{carlini} of a chunk's bytes evades the hash. This drops recall to
\RTrecallDriftThree{} at 30\% drift and \RTrecallDriftFive{} at 50\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}a). Closing this gap needs near-duplicate or semantic
matching~\cite{broder1997minhash,charikar2002simhash,indyk1998lsh}, the central
piece of future work. We do not claim per-chunk signed provenance as novel:
prior and concurrent systems already sign provenance per embedding, per answer,
per dataset, or per audio chunk~\cite{VectorPin,leray2025policy,ZKPROV,MerkleSpeech}.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} E2's suspects and labels come from one released
attack-feedback set over NQ. The corpus-scale results (E3) use the full
\FullPassages{}-passage corpus, and the structural arguments, constant work for
lookup and revocation, do not depend on scale. The two baselines are the
published RAGForensics and RAGOrigin procedures, run over local open models
rather than the hosted models the original papers used. We report our own
measured recall, precision, latency, and model-call count on identical inputs.
Adaptive and multi-attacker regimes, and other corpora such as HotpotQA and
MS-MARCO, are future work. We assume a trusted operator holding the private key;
key compromise is a residual risk.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Corpus poisoning is cheap and effective. Standards demand
provenance and quarantine. Yet the closest tools either filter at query time or
attribute after the fact, with no recovery step. We opened with an operational
question: once a source is found compromised, how precisely and how cheaply can
an operator purge it? \sysname{} answers it. By sealing per-chunk signed
provenance at ingestion, it revokes a compromised source surgically, removing
exactly its chunks at a false-purge rate of \textbf{\PerChunkFP{}} where
document-level purging discards \PerDocFP{} of what it removes. It also turns
traceback into a content-hash lookup that costs no model calls. The recovery is
cheap because the index does the work, but the value is the clean excision, not
the speed. The limit is equally concrete. The exact-hash basis means \sysname{}
does not hold against a content-mutating adversary, with recall falling to
\RTrecallDriftThree{} under 30\% post-ingestion drift, and it presupposes an
external detector or analyst to flag the source. Pairing this recovery layer with
near-duplicate matching is the natural next step. We release \sysname{} as a
reproducible, double-blind
artifact.\footnote{\href{https://anonymous.4open.science}{Anonymized artifact
repository, for double-blind review.}}
